Read the abstract

Reliable evaluation of open-ended question answering remains a bottleneck for measuring answer correctness of modern LLMs. Unlike multiple-choice tasks, free-form answers may be correct in many surface forms and may fail in qualitatively different ways, including incompleteness, contradiction, overgeneration, and endorsement of false premises. Existing judgment-based and similarity-based metrics often collapse these distinctions. We address this gap with three reusable contributions. First, we introduce a semantic correctness taxonomy that assigns open-ended answers to eight ordered classes, separating verbose-but-correct answers from those contaminated by hallucinated content. Second, we release CAP-Correctness, an 8.8k-example benchmark spanning widely used QA datasets, and CAP-Statements, an 11k-example dataset for converting question-answer pairs into declarative statements for natural language inference (NLI) training and statement-based evaluation. Third, we introduce CAP (Context-Aware Precision), a reference-based metric that scores question-conditioned statements using bidirectional NLI. Under a monotonicity protocol testing whether metrics respect the taxonomy's intended ordering, CAP outperforms established baselines.

Taxonomy

An eight-class correctness taxonomy

Each answer gets one of eight classes by how it relates to the gold answer under the same question. A good metric should score them in this order.

📐 Monotonicity target (Eq. 1)
  • Separates verbose answers: overinclusive-valid adds correct extra information, overinclusive-invalid adds hallucinated information.
  • Separates failure modes: incomplete (partial), wrong (invalid) and contradictory answers.

Method

How CAP works

CAP turns the question with each answer into a declarative statement, then asks an NLI model whether the two statements entail each other, in both directions.

📐 CAP (Eqs. 2–3)
\[ \mathbf{D}(s_a \to s_b) = P_{\text{entail}}(s_a \to s_b) + \lambda\, P_{\text{neutral}}(s_a \to s_b) \]
\[ \text{CAP} = \alpha\, \mathbf{D}(s_g \to s_p) + (1-\alpha)\, \mathbf{D}(s_p \to s_g), \qquad \alpha = 0.85,\ \lambda = 0.3 \]

A convex combination of two scores in [0, 1], so CAP always lies in [0, 1].

Use CAP on your data

An online scorer is coming soon. Until then, CAP runs locally with the Python package.

score_with_cap.pyPython
# pip install git+https://github.com/ElitsaY/Correctness_eval.git
from cap_eval import CAPScorer

# Statement pairs: (gold statement, answer statement)
scorer = CAPScorer()
scorer.score(["Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius."],
             ["The boiling point of water is one hundred degrees Celsius."])

# Raw (question, gold, answer) triples, with the statement model
scorer = CAPScorer(statement_model="path/to/mt5-qa-statement")
scorer.score_answers(["At what temperature does water boil?"],
                     ["100 degrees Celsius"], ["one hundred degrees Celsius"])

Summary: key takeaways

  1. Correctness is ordinal, not binary: eight classes separate paraphrases, verbose answers, partial answers and contradictions.
  2. Similarity metrics miss the order: BLEU, ROUGE-L, METEOR and BERTScore stay near chance in pairwise accuracy and put about half of the class pairs out of order.
  3. CAP follows the taxonomy: Spearman 60.4 vs. 26.9 for the best baseline, with 4 of 25 class pairs out of order.
  4. Known failure modes: partial answers score too high and alternative-correct answers too low, both artefacts of whole-statement NLI.
